Part 1 · Completeness: what counts as missing?

With the class · about 15 minutes

Key idea

"Missing" is written in at least six ways

When a nurse had no value to enter, they did one of several things: left the cell empty, typed NULL, typed N/A or NA, typed 999, or typed Unknown. Different people, different sites, different days. All of them mean the same thing: there is no value here.

The computer does not know that. R and Python each have their own built-in list of words that mean "missing", and the lists are different. Neither of them thinks 999 or Unknown means missing. So for the D-dimer blood test, the same file gives different answers to "how many values are missing?" depending on which tool you use and how it is set up:

0R (read.csv as used here): blanks are kept as text
25actually missing, once you count all six markers

So the first job is not to count. It is to write down what you mean by missing, as a list, and then use that one list everywhere. That list is the first line of your transformation log in Part 5.

Key idea

Spread out, or concentrated?

"25% of D-dimer values are missing" sounds like a nuisance. But a single percentage hides the question that matters: is the missingness spread evenly across all patients, or is it piled up in one place?

If it is piled up at one hospital, then throwing away patients with a missing value throws away that hospital. The way to find out is to split the table by site and count again. (In R this is tapply(); in Python, groupby(). Both mean "do the same calculation for each group".)

Going further: three reasons data goes missing
Read

Statisticians give the reasons data goes missing three names. You will meet them in the reading; you are not tested on them here, but they explain why your answer to the question above matters so much.

  • Missing completely at random: a form was dropped in a puddle. The missing values are a random sample, and leaving them out loses some precision but does not bias the result.
  • Missing at random (given what you do know): Wirral Park's lab does not routinely run D-dimer, so the gap depends on site, which is recorded. You can allow for it, if you know the reason.
  • Missing not at random: the test was only ordered for the sickest patients, so the gap depends on the very thing you are trying to predict. This is the dangerous one, and the data alone cannot tell you it is happening.

The practical point: a pattern of missingness is a question to take to the people who collected the data, not something to fix in code.

Part 3 · Plausibility: could this value be true?

In pairs · about 13 minutes

Key idea

Well-formed is not the same as believable

Conformance asked "is it the right shape?". A temperature of 3.7 is a perfectly good number. Plausibility asks the next question: could a living patient have this value?

The usual check is a range for each measurement, written down by someone who knows the clinical meaning: body temperature between 34 and 42 °C, oxygen saturation between 50 and 100%, and so on. Anything outside the range is flagged, not deleted. The ranges below are decisions, and a clinician could argue with every one of them.

One thing to watch for: in Part 1 you listed 999 as a way of writing "missing". If a column is turned into numbers before that list is applied, 999 turns into a real number and gets flagged as an impossible weight. That is a mistake in the code, not in the data.

Part 4 · Uniqueness: is anyone in here twice?

In pairs · about 10 minutes

Key idea

Finding a duplicate is a decision, not a mechanical step

Two records are "the same person" only if you can show it. The obvious way is to look for two rows with the same name and date of birth. But names get misspelt and dates get typed in different formats, so a match on those can miss a real duplicate. An identifier that is meant to be unique to a person, like the NHS number, is a better key. This is the same problem as record linkage between two files, just inside one file.

Part 5 · Write down what you did

After the break · in pairs · about 25 minutes

Key idea

Raw stays raw; every change is logged

The file as you received it is the raw copy (some teams call it the bronze layer). It is never edited. Your cleaned version is a second copy (the silver layer). Between the two sits a list of rules, and every rule goes in a transformation log with the number of rows or values it changed.

Without the log, nobody (including you, in six months) can tell a real value from one you corrected, and nobody can check your work. The log is short and plain: what you did, to which field, how many it affected, and what you assumed.

Key idea

The datasheet: a label for the dataset

Food comes with a label listing what is in it. Gebru and colleagues (2021) argued that every dataset should too: a datasheet answering fixed questions about where the data came from, what is in it, what has been done to it, and what it should and should not be used for. The full version has about fifty questions. You will write six.

Write it for a researcher who has never seen NORTHSTAR and has ten minutes to decide whether to apply for it. Every answer should be a fact you can point to: in the data, the dictionary, the study information sheet, or today's outputs. Where you do not know, write not known and say who would.

Datasheet

Datasheet for NORTHSTAR

What is in it. What does one record represent, how many are there, and roughly what groups of fields (identifiers, demographics, admission, bloods, daily observations, outcome, free text)?
Where it came from. Why was it collected, by whom, over what period, and under what consent? Does the consent cover a model like this one?
What is missing. The two worst fields, with numbers, and whether the gaps are concentrated at one site.
