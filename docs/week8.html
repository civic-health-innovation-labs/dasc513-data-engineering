Task 1 · Mini data protection impact assessment

In pairs · about 30 minutes

Key idea

A DPIA is a written answer to "what could this do to the people in the data, and what are we doing about it?"

The law (UK GDPR Article 35) requires one whenever a use of personal data is likely to put individuals at high risk. The ICO keeps a list of things that count as high risk, and this project hits three of them: health data on a large scale, linking datasets together, and using new technology such as machine learning.

The risks are to the people in the data, not to your project. "The model might not work" is not a DPIA risk. "Patients at the smaller hospital are missing from the training data, so the model will be worse for them" is.

The ICO's template has seven steps. This page compresses them into five short sections, and uses the ICO's own words for the scales:

ScaleThe wordsIn plain language
Likelihood of harmremote · possible · probableCould happen but unlikely · might well happen · expect it to happen
Severity of harmminimal · significant · severeA nuisance · real harm to some people · serious or lasting harm
Overall risklow · medium · highYour judgement, combining the two
Effect of a measureeliminated · reduced · acceptedGone · smaller · still there, and you are saying so
DPIA A

A. What and why

Why a DPIA is needed: which of the ICO's high-risk triggers apply to this project
What happens to the data, in one short paragraph: where it comes from, where it goes, who sees it, how long it is kept. Refer to your week 6 diagram; do not redraw it.
Who the people are and what they were told. What did participants agree to (the week 5 provenance sheet and the study information)? Would they expect this use? Are any of them vulnerable?
Before you answer

Three permissions, not one

Using health data for research in England needs three separate things to line up. You met them in the lecture; here they are in one place so you can choose, not memorise.

PermissionWhat it isWhat a project like this usually relies on
Lawful basis (UK GDPR Article 6)The reason the law allows you to use personal data at allFor a university or NHS research project: public task, Article 6(1)(e). Consent is rarely used as the GDPR basis for research, because it can be withdrawn and must be freely given.
Special category condition (Article 9)Health data needs an extra reason on top of Article 6Research in the public interest, Article 9(2)(j), with the safeguards in the Data Protection Act 2018 (Schedule 1, paragraph 4)
Common law duty of confidentialityA separate rule: what a patient tells a clinician stays confidential unless there is a route outOne of three routes: the patient consented to this use; section 251 support from the Confidentiality Advisory Group; or the data is anonymised so the duty no longer applies

The useful question for NORTHSTAR is the third one: look at what the consent form covered (week 5), and at whether the data the researcher sees is anonymised (week 6). Pick the route that is actually true, and say why in one line.

DPIA B

B. Permission and proportionality

Lawful basis and special category condition. Which Article 6 basis and which Article 9 condition, and one line on why they fit
Common law route: consent, section 251 support, or anonymised? Which one is actually true for this project, and why
Could the purpose be achieved with less? Fewer people, fewer fields, less identifiable data, no linkage, a different setting. Name one thing you are not taking.
How people could find out or object. Does the national data opt-out apply to any part of this? Who would a participant ask?
Before you answer

Write three risks. For each one, name the source (what could go wrong) and the harm to people (what it would do to them), rate it, then say what you would do and what is left. At least one risk must come from your week 7 data quality findings: a model trained on data that leaves out or misrepresents some patients is a risk to those patients, not just a technical problem.

Task 2 · Data access application

Same pairs · about 30 minutes

Key idea

Ask for the least that answers the question

This form is a short version of what real committees ask for (NHS England's Data Access Request Service, UK Biobank, CPRD and SAIL all ask broadly the same things). The part they read most carefully is the field-by-field table: for every field you want, why the question needs it, and in the least identifiable form that still works.

The "minimum form" of a field is the least revealing version that still does the job:

Minimum formMeansExample
rawAs recordedTemperature on admission
bandedPut into groupsAge band instead of date of birth; month instead of date
derivedReplaced by something calculated from itDeprivation decile from the postcode; days since onset from two dates
linkage onlyUsed to join to another dataset, then removed; never analysedNHS number, replaced by a study ID before the researcher sees it
droppedNot requestedPatient name

A good application always gives something up. If every field is "raw", the committee will ask why.

Section 1

1. What, why, and for whom

The research question, in one sentence
What will come out: tables, figures, a model, code, a paper. Will patients be told what was found?
Who benefits, and how. Be specific. "Improving patient care" is not a public benefit statement. "A triage model that flags the admissions at highest risk of death within 48 hours, evaluated at three sites, so that critical care review can be prioritised" is.

Permission: the committee reads section B of your DPIA for this. Make sure the application and the DPIA say the same thing.

Task 3 · You are the data access committee

Two pairs together, after the break · about 25 minutes

Key idea

The committee does not judge the science

You have another pair's DPIA and application (swap downloaded files, or swap laptops). Your job is to decide whether this use of these people's data, by these applicants, in this setting, with these outputs is justified, and to say so in writing in a way the applicant can act on. Appoint a chair, who breaks ties and signs the letter.

Real committees most often say approve with conditions. A good condition is something the applicant can actually do, that someone can check, with a date. A reason that could be sent to any application ("the committee has concerns about data minimisation") is noise; a reason that names a field and a section is a decision.

Score each criterion from 0 to 3: 0 absent or wrong · 1 stated but not explained · 2 explained with gaps · 3 explained, specific, and consistent with the rest of the application.

Committee A

Part A. Is it complete? (about five minutes)

Lay summary is present and a patient panel could understand it
A lawful basis, an Article 9 condition and a common law route are all named (in the DPIA, section B)
Every field group has a minimum form and a reason
At least one field has been dropped, with a reason
A safe setting is named and a rule for outputs is given
The DPIA refers to the same project, and at least one of its risks is a data quality risk

Committee B

Part B. Is it justified?

1. The permissions make senseThe lawful basis fits a research project; the common law route matches what participants were actually told and whether the data is really anonymised
2. Minimisation is argued, not assertedEach field's reason refers to the research question; minimum forms are used where they would work; direct identifiers are linkage-only or dropped; something has been given up
3. The DPIA matches the applicationSame fields, linkage, setting and outputs; the DPIA's risks include a data quality risk, not only security; residual risks are stated rather than claimed away
4. Benefit and safeguards are specificNames who benefits and how; a named environment with real constraints; output types listed with a disclosure rule and a checker; someone outside the team has been asked

A guide, not a formula: 10 to 12 usually approve; 7 to 9 usually approve with conditions; below 7 usually reject. A 0 on permissions or on the DPIA match is a reject whatever the total.
